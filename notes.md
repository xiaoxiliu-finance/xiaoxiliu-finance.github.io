  <h2 class="commentary-title">
    When Weak Firms Survive Through Weak Banks
  </h2>
 
  <div class="commentary-epigraph">
    <em>When weak firms survive through weak banks, stagnation crosses borders.</em>
    
  </div>
  <span class="commentary-meta">March 2026</span>
  
  <div class="commentary-body">
    
  In many Asian economies, zombie firms have quietly become a structural feature rather than a cyclical anomaly.
      These are mature firms unable to cover debt-servicing costs, yet kept alive through continued credit access.
      While often framed as a micro-level inefficiency, their persistence generates distortions
      that travel far beyond their home economies—through the prices of manufactured goods, not financial markets.

  The domestic mechanism is familiar. Economies with a higher zombie share experience weaker GDP growth
      and persistent disinflation. Zombie firms, sustained by evergreened credit, maintain production
      at the cost of lower markups, creating excess capacity that pushes prices down.
      Capital and labor remain tied to low-productivity firms, crowding out healthier competitors.

  But the distortion crosses borders. Advanced economies more exposed to Asian trade experience lower
      inflation and slower growth when Asian zombie shares rise—driven by underpriced intermediate inputs
      from non-China Asian exporters feeding into downstream production costs abroad.
      The banking channel, by contrast, does not transmit: foreign banks absorb zombie exposure
      through tighter monitoring without balance sheet deterioration, insulating the international
      financial system from zombie risk.

  Zombification is therefore not merely a firm-level inefficiency.
      It is a macro-financial dynamic whose domestic distortions spill internationally through supply chains,
      arriving in advanced economies as a disinflationary force beyond the reach of domestic monetary policy—
      and outside the perimeter of bank-focused financial surveillance.
  
  </div>
