  <h2 class="commentary-title">
    When Weak Firms Survive Through Weak Banks
  </h2>
 
  <div class="commentary-epigraph">
    <em>When weak firms survive through weak banks, stagnation crosses borders.</em>
    
  </div>
  <span class="commentary-meta">March 2026</span>
  
  <div class="commentary-body">
    
  In many Asian economies, zombie firms have quietly become a structural feature rather than a cyclical anomaly.
      These are mature firms unable to cover debt-servicing costs, yet kept alive through continued credit access.
      While often framed as a micro-level inefficiency, their persistence generates distortions
      that travel far beyond their home economies—through the prices of manufactured goods, not financial markets.

  The domestic mechanism is familiar. Economies with a higher zombie share experience weaker GDP growth
      and persistent disinflation. Zombie firms, sustained by evergreened credit, maintain production
      at the cost of lower markups, creating excess capacity that pushes prices down.
      Capital and labor remain tied to low-productivity firms, crowding out healthier competitors.

  But the distortion crosses borders. Advanced economies more exposed to Asian trade experience lower
      inflation and slower growth when Asian zombie shares rise—driven by underpriced intermediate inputs
      from non-China Asian exporters feeding into downstream production costs abroad.
      The banking channel, by contrast, does not transmit: foreign banks absorb zombie exposure
      through tighter monitoring without balance sheet deterioration, insulating the international
      financial system from zombie risk.

  Zombification is therefore not merely a firm-level inefficiency.
      It is a macro-financial dynamic whose domestic distortions spill internationally through supply chains,
      arriving in advanced economies as a disinflationary force beyond the reach of domestic monetary policy—
      and outside the perimeter of bank-focused financial surveillance.
  
  </div>

  <h2 class="commentary-title">
    Where Capital Flows, and Why It Stays
  </h2>

  <div class="commentary-epigraph">
    <em>Network position in global finance is not earned each cycle—it is inherited.</em>
  </div>
  <span class="commentary-meta">March 2026</span>

  <div class="commentary-body">

  The architecture of global capital flows is less fluid than it appears.
      Behind the daily movement of cross-border investment lies a network structure
      that changes slowly, concentrates persistently, and confers durable advantages
      on a small number of hub economies. Understanding who sits at the center of that network—
      and what it means to be there—is increasingly central to financial stability analysis.

  Network centrality carries real macroeconomic consequences, but they are asymmetric.
      Hub countries experience lower GDP growth on average, but also lower volatility and
      greater resilience during crises. The composition of centrality matters as much as its level:
      countries more central as capital recipients face higher banking crisis probability,
      while creditor centrality and FDI hub status tend to reduce risk.
      Portfolio hub status—exposed to volatile flows and contagion from multiple directions—destabilizes;
      FDI hub status stabilizes.

  The United States occupies a position that fundamentally defies gravity model predictions.
      After accounting for economic size, distance, institutions, and openness, the US attracts
      far more portfolio investment and FDI than any benchmark would suggest, and this premium
      has grown since 2015. Portfolio investment globally has become increasingly concentrated
      on both sides: a narrowing set of creditor hubs supplies capital, and an ever-larger share
      flows to a single debtor. Europe's position as both source and destination has eroded steadily,
      with the gap absorbed by the US on the receiving end and by Asia Pacific on the supplying side.

  This concentration reshapes how we should read external imbalances. Current account balances
      and gross financial positions have decoupled: while current accounts have remained relatively
      stable, gross external assets and liabilities have more than doubled relative to world GDP
      over the past two decades, and their cross-sectional correlation is close to zero.
      Network centrality—not the current account—predicts gross position accumulation,
      and Granger causality runs from centrality to gross stocks, not the reverse.
      A country's standing in the network determines how much capital flows through it;
      the current account merely records what is left over.

  The deeper implication is structural rigidity. Network position is largely determined by
      historical and path-dependent factors that neither institutional reform nor capital account
      liberalization can easily shift. For financial stability surveillance, the practical
      consequence is clear: gross positions and network centrality are the more reliable lens
      for identifying where vulnerabilities are building—not current account balances,
      which increasingly miss the financial globalization they purport to measure.

  </div>
